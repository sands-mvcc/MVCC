    The feedback loop

    A controller does one thing over and over: measure, compare, correct.

    The transmitter measures the process variable (PV), such as tank level, and sends it to the controller, usually as a 4–20 mA or digital signal. The controller subtracts PV from the setpoint (SP) to get the error. It then calculates a controller output (CO), typically 0–100%, that drives a final control element such as a valve, damper or variable-speed drive.

    Something is always trying to push the process away from setpoint. These disturbances (a change in demand, feed temperature or supply pressure) are why we need closed-loop control at all.

    Think of driving at a steady speed. Your eyes read the speedometer (transmitter), your brain compares it to the speed limit (setpoint), and your foot adjusts the throttle (final element). A hill is the disturbance.

    Proportional: react to the present error

    The P term moves the output in proportion to how far PV is from setpoint right now.

    P = Kc × e

    Double the error and the P correction doubles. A higher controller gain Kc gives a stronger, faster response. Many controllers express this instead as proportional band: PB = 100 ÷ Kc, so a PB of 50% is a gain of 2.

    Proportional-only control has a built-in weakness. The P term only produces output when there is error, so when a load change requires a different valve position, the loop settles with a permanent offset between PV and SP. Raising the gain shrinks the offset, but too much gain causes oscillation, especially when the process has dead time.

    Integral: remove the past error

    The I term adds up error over time and keeps moving the output until the error is gone.

    I = (Kc ÷ Ti) × ∫ e dt

    As long as any error remains, integral keeps pushing, so it eliminates offset. The integral time Ti is how long it takes integral action to repeat the proportional correction. A smaller Ti means stronger integral. Some vendors use repeats per minute (60 ÷ Ti in seconds) instead; check which units your controller uses before changing anything.

    Too much integral makes the loop overshoot and oscillate slowly. If the output hits 0% or 100% while error persists, the integral keeps growing anyway. That is integral windup, and it causes a large overshoot later. Anti-reset windup logic prevents it.

    Derivative: anticipate the future

    The D term reacts to how fast the PV is changing, braking the response before it overshoots.

    D = −Kc × Td × dPV/dt

    When PV is racing toward setpoint, derivative pulls the output back early. On slow processes with dead time, such as temperature, a modest derivative time Td allows a higher gain with less overshoot.

    Derivative amplifies noise. On fast, noisy loops like flow and pressure it makes the valve chatter, so it is usually left off. Most modern controllers take the derivative of PV rather than error, which avoids a large output spike, the derivative kick, every time the operator changes the setpoint.

    Putting it together

    The output is the sum of three corrections: present, past and future.

    CO = Kc [ e + (1/Ti) ∫ e dt + Td de/dt ] + bias

    This is the standard (ISA) form used in this simulator. Other forms exist. In the parallel form each term has its own independent gain, and the series (interacting) form is common in older analog and some PLC controllers. The same numbers produce different behavior in different forms, so always confirm the algorithm before copying tuning values between systems.

    • P provides the main, immediate correction.
    • I finishes the job and removes offset. Nearly every loop uses it.
    • D is optional. Use it on slow loops with lag; skip it on noisy fast loops.

    In practice most industrial loops run as PI. Watch the Live response bars in the simulator to see how each term contributes moment by moment.

    Tuning safely

    Tuning changes how a real plant behaves. Treat every change as a controlled change.

    • Get permission first. Follow your site's management-of-change and work-permit process and coordinate with operations.
    • Record the existing settings (Kc, Ti, Td, action, limits) so you can restore them.
    • Run a bump test. In manual, step the output a small amount and record the process gain K, dead time θ and time constant τ.
    • Start conservative. The lambda method sets Ti = τ and Kc = τ ÷ [K (λ + θ)]. A larger λ gives a slower, more robust loop.
    • Change one parameter at a time, then test with a small setpoint step and watch the trend.
    • Check the hardware. A sticky valve, a bad transmitter or the wrong controller action cannot be tuned away.
    • Document and protect. Log the final values. Tuning parameters are a cybersecurity asset: restrict who can change them and monitor for unauthorized edits.
