    Why the conversation has shifted

    IntegrationThe question is no longer whether students use AI but how, when and with what disclosure.
    EvidenceDetection-led enforcement has proven unreliable; process evidence and conversation hold up better.
    RegulationFrameworks such as the EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF and UNESCO guidance now treat education uses of AI as ethically significant.
    EquityPolicies and tools that ignore access gaps and detector bias can harm the students we most want to serve.

    Ethical foundations

    Most AI ethics frameworks converge on a small set of principles. Floridi and Cowls (2019) synthesized dozens of them into five: four borrowed from bioethics plus one new one, explicability, which AI demands.

    Frameworks shaping AI in education

    Integrity is a set of values, not a rule

    The International Center for Academic Integrity (ICAI) defines academic integrity through six fundamental values. AI changes the tools; it doesn’t change these.

    HonestyBeing truthful about how work was produced, including AI’s role.
    TrustClear expectations so students and faculty can rely on each other.
    FairnessConsistent, evidence-based processes that don’t disadvantage any group.
    RespectFor students’ privacy, voices and the work of others, human or machine-assisted.
    ResponsibilityOwning the accuracy of anything you submit or teach, whoever drafted it.
    CourageHolding standards and admitting uncertainty while practice evolves.

    Detection tools and their limits

    An AI-detector score is a probability estimate from a classifier, not evidence of what a student did. Understanding why requires a little statistics and a look at the research.

    61%Average share of TOEFL essays by non-native English writers that seven detectors misclassified as AI-generated.Liang et al., 2023, Patterns
    14 toolsTested by an international team; none were both accurate and reliable, and all scored below 80% accuracy.Weber-Wulff et al., 2023, Int. J. for Educational Integrity
    26%True-positive rate OpenAI reported for its own AI-text classifier before withdrawing it in July 2023 for low accuracy.OpenAI, 2023
    ParaphraseLight rewording by another model was enough to defeat several detection approaches in controlled tests.Sadasivan et al., 2023, University of Maryland

    What a detector score can’t tell you

    • Whether AI was used for brainstorming, grammar help or full drafting.
    • Whether the student’s use was permitted by your policy.
    • Whether a fluent, formulaic or second-language writing style triggered it.
    • Whether the text was paraphrased or lightly edited to evade it.
    • What the student actually understands.
    Legal and equity exposureRelying on a score that falls disproportionately on non-native English speakers or disabled students can raise concerns under Title VI and the ADA/Section 504. Many institutions now direct faculty never to use a detector score as the sole basis for a misconduct finding.

    A better evidence ladder

    If something feels off, climb from the least to the most formal step.

    1. Re-read against your policy. Was the expectation for this assignment clear and specific?
    2. Look at process evidence. Drafts, notes, version history, lab logs, prompt logs if disclosure was required.
    3. Have a curious conversation. Ask the student to walk you through their choices and sources.
    4. Check understanding live. A short oral explanation or extension task on the same content.
    5. Follow your institution’s process. Only then, and with documented evidence beyond any tool output.

    A short history of the detection problem

    1. Nov 2022 ChatGPT’s public release prompts a rush to adopt AI-text detectors.
    2. Apr 2023 Turnitin enables AI-writing detection across its user base.
    3. Jul 2023 OpenAI withdraws its own AI classifier, citing low accuracy. Liang et al. publish evidence of bias against non-native English writers.
    4. Aug 2023 Several universities, including Vanderbilt, disable AI detection, citing false-positive risk and lack of transparency.
    5. Dec 2023 Weber-Wulff et al. find none of 14 tools reliable enough for high-stakes use.
    6. 2024 onward Practice shifts toward disclosure, process evidence, assessment redesign and frameworks such as the AI Assessment Scale.

    Build your course AI policy

    A good policy tells students what’s allowed, why, how to disclose it and what happens if something goes wrong. Build a draft, then adapt it to your institution’s language.

    Define it

    Name the tools and uses you mean. “AI” alone is ambiguous: spellcheck, Grammarly and ChatGPT are all AI.

    Scope it

    Set a default level and allow assignment-level exceptions.

    Explain it

    Tie the rule to a learning outcome. Students follow rules they understand.

    Disclose it

    Say exactly how to document AI use, and make disclosure safe, not self-incriminating.

    Protect data

    Tell students not to paste personal, confidential or others’ work into AI tools.

    Plan for concerns

    Describe a conversation-first process that doesn’t rely on detector scores.

    Redesign assessment for real learning

    “AI-proof” assignments don’t exist. The goal is assessment where the evidence of learning is hard to fake and where any permitted AI use is visible and purposeful.

    Audit an assignment

    0

      Before and after

      Pick a discipline to see a conventional assignment rebuilt around process, context and live demonstration.

      Principles

      • Process over product. Grade drafts, decisions and reflection, not only the final artifact.
      • Context AI doesn’t have. Local data, personal experience, class discussions, lab results.
      • Live moments. Brief orals, demos, whiteboard walkthroughs, in-class checkpoints.
      • Name AI’s role. Every assignment states its level on the scale.

      Watch for unintended harm

      • Timed in-class writing can disadvantage students with disabilities; honor accommodations.
      • Oral checks can stress anxious or multilingual students; keep them low-stakes and conversational.
      • “Personal experience” prompts can pressure disclosure; always offer an alternative.

      Disclosure and citation

      Transparency is the backbone of ethical AI use, for students and for faculty. Generate a disclosure statement plus APA and MLA style references, then model the same practice in your own course materials.

      How it was used

      Formats follow the general patterns published by APA Style (2023) and MLA (2023). Check the current guidance and your institution’s preferred style before requiring a format.

      Faculty disclosure matters tooIf you use AI to draft quiz items, feedback or slides, say so in the syllabus. Students notice double standards, and modeling verification (“I checked these questions against the textbook”) teaches the habit you want.
      9

      Teaching AI literacy

      Students rarely learn responsible AI use by being told what not to do. Teach a repeatable loop, then practice it on course content.

      Key terms

      Select a card to flip it.

      Classroom activities

      • Hallucination hunt. Students ask AI for five sources on a course topic and verify each one.
      • Bias probe. Compare outputs for prompts that differ only in a name or location.
      • Critique the bot. Grade an AI answer using your own rubric.
      • Prompt postmortem. Share a prompt that failed and explain why.

      Questions to gauge readiness

      • Can you explain why an AI tool might state something false with confidence?
      • Do you know what this course allows and how to disclose it?
      • What information should never go into a public AI tool?
      • Can you use AI to extend your thinking rather than replace it?
