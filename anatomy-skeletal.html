Warm-up from the tissues lesson

Bone and cartilage are both connective tissues. What do all connective tissues have in common, and what makes bone hard?

Answer: cells scattered in an extracellular matrix of fibers and ground substance. In bone, collagen fibers give flexibility and calcium phosphate crystals (hydroxyapatite) give hardness. Cartilage matrix stays firm but flexible and has no blood vessels.

Part 1

Axial or appendicular?

The axial skeleton protects and supports the head, neck and trunk. The appendicular skeleton moves you: limbs plus the pectoral and pelvic girdles that anchor them to the axial skeleton. Watch the girdles; they are the most common mistake.

Part 2

Reading bone landmarks

Landmarks (bone markings) are bumps, ridges, holes and smooth surfaces. Their names tell you their job: rough projections anchor muscles and ligaments, smooth rounded projections form joints, and openings let vessels and nerves pass. The femur shows many of them in one bone.

Part 3

Compact and spongy bone

A long bone is a tube of dense compact bone capped by ends filled with spongy bone. Compact bone is built from osteons that run along the length of the bone. Spongy bone is a lattice of trabeculae with red marrow in the spaces, light but strong along lines of stress.

Part 4

Three kinds of cartilage

Cartilage is firm, flexible and avascular: it has no blood vessels, so it heals slowly. Most is wrapped in a perichondrium that supplies it by diffusion. Chondrocytes sit in small spaces called lacunae. The fibers in the matrix decide the type.

Part 5

Classifying joints

Every joint gets two labels. The structural class says what holds the bones together. The functional class says how much the joint moves. Structure predicts function: the more tightly bones are bound, the less they move.

Key takeaways

  • Two divisions. 80 axial bones protect the central body; 126 appendicular bones, including both girdles, move it.
  • Landmark names are job descriptions. Rough projections anchor muscles, smooth projections form joints, openings carry vessels and nerves.
  • Compact outside, spongy inside. Osteons give strength along the shaft; trabeculae give light strength at the ends and hold red marrow.
  • Cartilage follows its fibers. Hyaline is smooth, elastic bends and springs back, fibrocartilage resists compression.
  • Shape sets movement. Fibrous and cartilaginous joints move little or not at all; synovial joint shapes range from gliding to multiaxial.
