    The body’s largest organ, layer by layer

    Your skin weighs about 4 kg and covers nearly 2 m². It is a waterproof shield, a thermostat and a sense organ, and every one of those jobs depends on what sits at which depth.

    By the end of this lesson you can

    Identify the skin’s layers and accessory structures, and explain how their arrangement supports protection and sensation.

    Part 1

    Layers of the skin

    The skin (cutaneous membrane) has two layers, the epidermis and the dermis. Beneath it lies the hypodermis, which anchors skin to the structures below but is not technically part of the skin.

    1.1

    Skin cross-section explorer

    Find every layer and accessory structure in one block of skin

    Read the block from the surface down. The epidermis is a thin sheet of stratified squamous epithelium with no blood vessels. The dermis beneath it is a thick, tough connective tissue that holds the blood vessels, nerves, glands and hair follicles. The hypodermis (subcutaneous layer) is mostly adipose tissue.

    Notice that most accessory structures are made of epidermal cells that have grown down into the dermis. A hair follicle and a sweat gland are both pockets of epidermis, which is why skin can regrow from them after a deep scrape.

    1.2

    The epidermis and its strata

    A sheet of cells that is always rebuilding itself from the bottom up

    The epidermis is keratinized stratified squamous epithelium. New cells form in the deepest layer and are pushed toward the surface, filling with the tough protein keratin and dying along the way. The trip takes roughly 25 to 45 days; the dead surface cells then flake off. Because the epidermis is avascular, its deepest cells live on nutrients diffusing from capillaries in the dermis, and cells farther from that supply die.

    Four cell types live here: keratinocytes (most cells; make keratin), melanocytes (make the pigment melanin), dendritic (Langerhans) cells (immune sentinels) and tactile (Merkel) cells (touch receptors paired with a nerve ending). Thick skin on the palms and soles has five strata; thin skin everywhere else has four.

    1.3

    The dermis and hypodermis

    The strong, living layer that feeds and anchors the epidermis

    The papillary dermis is a thin superficial layer of areolar connective tissue. Its fingerlike dermal papillae push up into the epidermis, locking the two layers together against shearing forces and bringing capillary loops and touch receptors close to the surface. On the palms and soles, papillae sit on top of larger ridges that produce friction ridges, the source of fingerprints.

    The reticular dermis, about 80% of the dermis, is dense irregular connective tissue. Its collagen bundles run in many directions, so skin resists tearing from any angle, while elastic fibers let it stretch and recoil. The hypodermis below is adipose and areolar tissue that stores fat, insulates, cushions and anchors skin loosely enough to slide over muscle.

    Part 2

    Accessory structures

    Hair, nails and skin glands are all derived from the epidermis, yet most of their working parts sit deep in the dermis.

    2.1

    Hair and nails

    Two keratin structures built by a matrix of dividing cells

    A hair is a column of dead, keratinized cells. The part above the surface is the shaft; the part within the follicle is the root. Growth happens in the hair matrix of the bulb, fed by capillaries in the dermal papilla. A hair follicle receptor (root hair plexus) wraps the bulb, so bending a hair is felt as touch. The arrector pili, a strip of smooth muscle, pulls the hair upright and squeezes the sebaceous gland beside it.

    A nail is a plate of hard keratin. It grows from the nail matrix under the proximal nail fold; the pale crescent of the lunule is the visible edge of that matrix. The nail protects the fingertip and stiffens it, which sharpens the sensitivity of the pulp beneath.

    2.2

    Skin glands

    Sebaceous glands and the sweat gland family

    Skin glands are exocrine: they release secretions through ducts. Sebaceous (oil) glands empty sebum into hair follicles. Sweat (sudoriferous) glands come in two main kinds, eccrine and apocrine, and two modified forms, ceruminous and mammary glands.

    SebaceousEverywhere except palms and solesInto a hair follicleOily sebum: softens hair and skin, slows water loss, kills some bacteria

    Part 3

    How arrangement supports function

    Every structure’s depth is a design decision. Here you test how the layers work together.

    3.1

    Stimulus lab: protection and sensation

    Apply a stimulus and watch which structures respond

    Sensory receptors are layered by what they detect. Receptors for fine touch sit shallow, in the dermal papillae and basal epidermis; receptors for deep pressure and vibration sit deep, in the reticular dermis and hypodermis. Protective layers are stacked the same way: a dead, waterproof surface, a living layer that shields DNA from UV, and a cushion of fat at the bottom.
