Earth Science 101: Oceans and their role in Earth’s systems

The ocean holds 97% of Earth’s water and trades heat, water, and carbon with the air and land every second. Its density, its currents, and its enormous heat capacity shape weather, climate, ecosystems, and coastlines. Work through the stations in order, or jump to the one your instructor assigns.

What you’ll be able to do
  • Use temperature and salinity to compare seawater densities and predict which water sinks. Station 1
  • Explain how winds drive surface gyres and how density differences drive deep circulation. Station 2
  • Interpret maps of currents, sea-surface temperature, and ocean depth, and explain how a current shapes a coastal climate. Station 3
  • Distinguish waves, tides, and currents, and interpret a tide graph. Station 4
  • Connect wave-driven sand transport and sea-level rise to shoreline change. Station 5
  • Explain how the ocean absorbs and redistributes heat and takes part in the carbon cycle. Station 6
Instructor guide (60–90 minute lab)
  • Station 1 (12 min): Students drag samples on the T–S diagram and predict sinking before pouring. Finish with the density-ranking challenge.
  • Station 2 (12 min): Turn Earth’s rotation off and on to discover western intensification. Then add meltwater until the overturning circulation weakens, and discuss what that means for high-latitude coasts.
  • Station 3 (15 min): Students switch between temperature and depth maps, drag a depth transect across the ridge, and compare the two harbor climographs before answering the interpretation questions.
  • Station 4 (12 min): Push a wave into shallow water until it breaks. Step through a lunar month on the tide graph and read the tidal range.
  • Station 5 (10 min): Predict where sand will pile up before adding a groin. Then raise sea level and count threatened houses.
  • Station 6 (10 min): Compare inland and coastal temperature ranges, then explore how temperature and CO₂ change ocean uptake and pH.
  • Station 7 (exit ticket): Ten auto-graded questions with explanations, plus vocabulary flip cards.

Circulation: wind on top, density below

Winds push the surface ocean into huge rotating gyres. Below, water that becomes cold and salty enough sinks and spreads along the bottom, a slow loop called the overturning (thermohaline) circulation.

Wind-driven gyre in an ocean basin (map view)

Particles are colored by speed: teal is slow, orange is fast. Arrows at right show the wind.

What to notice

  • Trade winds blow toward the west near the equator. Westerlies blow toward the east at mid-latitudes. Together they spin the gyre.
  • With Earth rotating, the flow crowds into a fast, narrow current on the west side of the basin (like the Gulf Stream or Kuroshio), with a slow, broad return flow elsewhere.
  • Gyres turn clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counterclockwise in the Southern Hemisphere.

Waves and tides: three different ways water moves

A wave moves energy, not water: each parcel loops in place. Tides are a slow, planet-scale rise and fall driven by the Moon and Sun. Currents carry water from place to place.

Shoreline change: sand on the move

Waves that reach the beach at an angle push sand along the shore in a zigzag (longshore drift). Anything that blocks that river of sand, or a rising sea, reshapes the coastline. This is a map view: the sea is at the top.

Sea-level rise rule of thumb (Bruun rule): a sandy shoreline retreats roughly 50–100 times the vertical rise. One meter of rise can mean 50–100 meters of lost beach.

Heat and carbon: the ocean as Earth’s buffer

Water takes about four times as much energy as dry rock or soil to warm by one degree. The ocean has absorbed over 90% of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases since the 1970s and about a quarter of the CO₂ people have emitted.

All three get the same sunlight. The deeper the layer of water that mixes and stores the heat, the smaller and later its seasonal swing. That is why coastal climates are milder than inland ones.

  • Solubility pump: cold water holds more CO₂ and carries it down where it sinks.
  • Biological pump: plankton use CO₂ in photosynthesis; their remains sink as “marine snow.”
  • More dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid, lowering pH and making shells harder to build.
