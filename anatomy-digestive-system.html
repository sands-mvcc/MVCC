Warm-up from the tissues lesson

Which epithelium would you expect to line a surface built for absorption, and which would you expect where food rubs hard against the wall?

Answer: simple columnar epithelium lines the stomach and intestines (thin enough to absorb and secrete), while stratified squamous epithelium lines the mouth, pharynx, esophagus and anal canal, where abrasion is high.

Part 1

Trace the path of food

The digestive tract (alimentary canal or GI tract) is one continuous tube. Food changes name as it travels: a bolus in the mouth and esophagus, chyme after the stomach, feces in the colon.

Tract organs or accessory organs?

The test is simple: does food physically pass through it? Tract organs form the tube. Accessory organs chew, or they make secretions that reach the tube through ducts.

Four layers, from esophagus to anal canal

From the lumen outward, the wall has the same plan everywhere: mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa, serosa. Regional differences (an extra muscle layer in the stomach, villi in the small intestine) are variations on this plan.

Part 4

Liver, gallbladder and pancreas

These three glands never touch food directly. Their secretions travel through a branching duct system and enter the duodenum at a single opening, the hepatopancreatic ampulla. Hormones from the duodenum decide when.

Duct check

Liver

▶

A liver lobule. Each corner holds a portal triad: a hepatic portal vein branch (blue), hepatic artery branch (red) and bile duct (green). Blood flows inward through sinusoids to the central vein; bile flows outward to the bile ducts.

  • Largest gland in the body, about 1.4 kg; right, left, caudate and quadrate lobes; the falciform ligament separates right and left lobes.
  • Hepatocytes make bile, about 0.5–1 L per day. Bile salts emulsify fat; bile also carries bilirubin out of the body.
  • Receives nutrient-rich blood from the intestines through the hepatic portal vein, then stores glycogen, iron and vitamins, makes plasma proteins such as albumin, and detoxifies drugs and alcohol.
  • Stellate macrophages (Kupffer cells) in the sinusoids remove bacteria and worn-out blood cells.

Gallbladder

▶
  • A thin-walled, pear-shaped sac about 10 cm long in a fossa on the inferior surface of the liver.
  • Stores bile and concentrates it by absorbing water. It does not make bile; the liver does.
  • When the hepatopancreatic sphincter is closed between meals, bile backs up the cystic duct into the gallbladder.
  • CCK makes the muscular wall contract and the sphincter relax, so bile flows to the duodenum when fat arrives.
  • Clinical link: overly concentrated bile can crystallize into gallstones that block the cystic or common bile duct.

Pancreas

▶
  • Retroperitoneal, behind the stomach; the head sits in the C-shaped curve of the duodenum and the tail reaches the spleen.
  • Exocrine acini (about 99% of the gland) make 1.2–1.5 L of pancreatic juice per day.
  • Secretin triggers bicarbonate-rich fluid that neutralizes acid chyme. CCK triggers enzymes: pancreatic amylase, lipase, nucleases and inactive proteases such as trypsinogen, which are activated only in the duodenum.
  • The main pancreatic duct joins the common bile duct at the hepatopancreatic ampulla, which opens on the major duodenal papilla.
  • Endocrine pancreatic islets release insulin and glucagon into the blood, not into the ducts.

Folds, villi and microvilli

Absorption happens across a surface, so the small intestine multiplies its surface at three scales. Switch each feature on to see what it adds.

0.5 m²

About the size of a desktop. This is the inner surface of a smooth tube 6 m long and 2.5 cm wide.

How big is it really? The ×3, ×10 and ×20 factors are textbook approximations; many books quote about 200 m², roughly a tennis court. A 2014 study that measured real tissue estimated closer to 30 m² for the small intestine. Either way, the folds multiply the surface many times over a plain tube.

Key takeaways

  • One tube. Mouth, pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum), large intestine (cecum, colon), rectum, anal canal.
  • Food never enters accessory organs. Teeth, tongue, salivary glands, liver, gallbladder and pancreas help from outside the path.
  • Same wall plan. Mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa, serosa (adventitia where there is no peritoneum).
  • One entrance for secretions. Bile and pancreatic juice meet at the hepatopancreatic ampulla; CCK and secretin control release.
  • Surface is everything. Circular folds, villi and microvilli multiply the absorptive area; lacteals take fats, capillaries take the rest.
