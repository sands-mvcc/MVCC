Greece and Rome

Greek city-states invented democracy, philosophy, and history as a form of inquiry. Rome built a republic, conquered the Mediterranean, became an empire, and left law, roads, and Latin across Europe. Both shaped the West, and both limited who counted as a full member of society.

Who gets a voice in governing, and what happens when power grows?

Big ideas

Greek political experiments asked who should rule; Roman expansion showed how conquest can hollow out a republic. Rome’s law, citizenship, and engineering held a vast empire together. In the west that empire fragmented in the fifth century, while in the east it lasted another thousand years.

    Athens and Sparta

    Greece was not one country but hundreds of independent city-states, each a polis. Athens and Sparta, the two most powerful, organized society in opposite ways. They fought together against Persia (490–479 BCE) and then against each other in the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE).

    Athens

    Democracy, sea power, trade, and the arts
    • Reforms of Solon (594 BCE) and Cleisthenes (508 BCE) built a direct democracy
    • Golden age under Pericles, c. 461–429 BCE
    • Wealth from silver mines, trade, and the Delian League

    Sparta

    Oligarchy, a land army, and military discipline
    • Mixed constitution: two kings, a council of elders, ephors, and an assembly
    • Citizens trained as soldiers from boyhood in the agoge
    • Economy rested on helots, a conquered population forced to farm

    In 509 BCE Romans expelled their king and created a republic designed so that no one person could hold too much power. Five centuries of conquest then strained that design until it broke.

      Two endings: West and East

      The empire was divided for administration and, after 395 CE, permanently between two emperors. The western half fragmented into Germanic kingdoms by 476. The eastern half, centered on Constantinople, continued as the Byzantine Empire until 1453.

      Western empireEastern (Byzantine) empireLost to other powers

      Why one half fell and the other survived

      The West

      Capital
      Rome, later Milan and Ravenna
      Economy
      Poorer, more rural; tax base shrank as land and trade were lost
      Pressures
      Long Rhine–Danube frontier; Visigoths, Vandals, and others settled inside the empire; Huns pushed peoples west
      Politics
      Child emperors and powerful Germanic generals; dependence on hired federate armies
      Outcome
      476: Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus. Germanic kingdoms kept Latin, Roman law, and the Church

      The East

      Capital
      Constantinople, founded by Constantine in 330
      Economy
      Wealthier, more urban; Egypt and Syria supplied grain and taxes
      Defenses
      Theodosian Walls and a site surrounded by water on three sides; strong navy
      Politics
      Continuous imperial government, bureaucracy, and a professional army
      Outcome
      Continued as the Byzantine Empire: Greek-speaking, Orthodox Christian, preserving Roman law until 1453
