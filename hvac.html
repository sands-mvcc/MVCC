Safety comes first

  • If you smell gas, leave the building. Don’t operate switches, phones or anything that could spark inside. Call the gas utility or 911 from outside.
  • Carbon monoxide (CO) has no color or odor. Every home with fuel-burning equipment needs working CO alarms.
  • Shut off and lock out power at the service switch before opening panels. The blower door switch is not a lockout.
  • Never jumper or bypass a pressure switch, limit or rollout switch to “get heat.” Find out why it opened.
  • Gas pressure adjustments and combustion testing are done by qualified technicians following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Your reference is the equipment

Acceptable readings and test procedures come from the furnace’s rating plate and service manual. Numbers in this lesson are typical values and the values used by the simulator’s virtual rating plate, not universal limits.

Two air paths, one heat exchanger

A furnace has two air paths. Combustion air feeds the burners, and the resulting flue gases pass through the inside of the heat exchanger and exit through the vent. Household air returns through the ductwork, passes across the outside of the heat exchanger, and goes back to the rooms through the supply ducts.

The heat exchanger transfers heat from one path to the other while keeping the streams separate. That separation is a safety barrier: a crack or hole can let flue gases, including carbon monoxide, mix with the air your customer breathes.

On a direct-vent (two-pipe) condensing furnace, combustion air comes through a dedicated outdoor intake. Other installations draw combustion air from the surrounding space, which must have adequate openings.

The normal heating sequence

The control board runs the same checklist every cycle. Each step must prove itself before the next one is allowed, and each has a defined failure response.

Timings vary by model. The simulator uses prepurge 15 s, igniter warm-up 17 s, trial for ignition 7 s, blower on-delay 30 s, postpurge 15 s and blower off-delay 120 s, with three ignition tries before lockout.

Cooling with the same air handler

A split-system air conditioner adds an evaporator coil on top of the furnace’s supply plenum and a condensing unit outdoors, connected by a refrigerant line set. The furnace still provides the filter, the blower and the ductwork, so airflow problems affect both heating and cooling.

  1. Call for cooling. The thermostat connects R to Y (compressor) and R to G (indoor fan).
  2. Outdoor unit starts. Y energizes the contactor coil in the condensing unit. The contactor closes the high-voltage contacts that start the compressor and condenser fan. Many thermostats and controls add an anti-short-cycle delay (often 3 to 5 minutes) after the compressor stops.
  3. Indoor blower runs at cooling speed. Cooling usually needs more airflow than heating, commonly around 350 to 400 CFM per ton.
  4. Refrigerant moves heat. The compressor raises refrigerant pressure and temperature. The condenser coil rejects heat outdoors. Liquid refrigerant passes through a metering device (often a TXV) into the evaporator coil, where it boils and absorbs heat from the house air. Cool vapor returns through the insulated suction line.
  5. Moisture drains. Air leaving the coil is cooler and drier. Water condenses on the coil, collects in the drain pan and leaves through a trapped drain line.
  6. Shutdown. When satisfied, Y and G drop, the outdoor unit stops and the blower may run a short off-delay.

Temperature split

The drop across the coil is often around 16 to 22 °F, depending on indoor humidity and airflow. A technician confirms charge with superheat or subcooling against the manufacturer’s charts. In the U.S., refrigerant work requires EPA Section 608 certification.

Furnace types and efficiency

AFUE (annual fuel utilization efficiency) is the share of fuel energy delivered as heat over a typical season. A 96% AFUE furnace turns about 96 cents of every fuel dollar into heat for the home.

Staging. A single-stage furnace fires at one rate. Two-stage furnaces usually start on low fire and move to high fire when needed. Modulating furnaces vary their firing rate in small steps. Staged and modulating units also change blower airflow to match the firing rate.

ENERGY STAR certification for gas furnaces requires high-efficiency condensing performance. U.S. federal minimum efficiency rules have been changing, so check current DOE standards and local codes when replacing equipment.

The maintenance check

A complete check follows the furnace from a thermostat call through ignition, steady operation and shutdown. During the run, the technician verifies combustion, venting and airflow, and compares every reading with the rating plate and service manual.

Before start-up

  • Read and record any stored fault code, then shut off power and gas.
  • Inspect or replace the filter.
  • Inspect burners, igniter, flame sensor and the visible heat exchanger.
  • Check vent and intake pipes and terminations; flush the condensate trap and drains.

During operation

  • Watch the full ignition sequence and the flame appearance.
  • Measure inlet and manifold gas pressure with a manometer.
  • Measure temperature rise and external static pressure.
  • Check flame signal and inducer pressure against the switch setpoint.
  • Perform a combustion analysis (CO air-free, O₂ or CO₂ per the manual).
  • In cooling season, measure the temperature split across the coil.

After

  • Watch a complete shutdown, including postpurge and the blower off-delay.
  • Test CO alarms, record readings and review findings with the customer.
