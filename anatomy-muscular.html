Skeletal muscle organization

A skeletal muscle is an organ built from bundles inside bundles. Each level is wrapped in connective tissue, and those wrappings blend together at the ends of the muscle to become the tendon. That continuity is why the pull of millions of tiny sarcomeres reaches the bone.

    Memory aid for the three wrappings

    Epimysium is “upon” the whole muscle. Perimysium goes “around” each fascicle. Endomysium is “within” the fascicle, around each single fiber. All three merge into the tendon at each end of the muscle.

    Tendons and attachments

    Most skeletal muscles attach to bone at two ends. The attachment that stays put is the origin; the one on the bone that moves is the insertion. When a muscle contracts, the insertion is pulled toward the origin.

    Three kinds of connective tissue “cables”

    StructureConnectsExample
    TendonMuscle to bone; a cord of dense regular connective tissueCalcaneal (Achilles) tendon: gastrocnemius and soleus to calcaneus
    AponeurosisMuscle to bone or to other muscle; a broad, flat sheet of tendonExternal oblique aponeurosis joining the linea alba
    LigamentBone to bone across a joint; not part of a musclePatellar ligament: patella to tibial tuberosity

    Rule of thumb: the origin is usually proximal (closer to the trunk) and the insertion is usually distal. In the biceps, the origin is on the scapula and the insertion is on the radius.

    When the “fixed” end moves: reversed action

    Origin and insertion describe the usual case. In a pull-up, your hands grip the bar, so the forearm is fixed and the biceps pulls your humerus and body upward toward the forearm. The muscle and its attachments haven’t changed; only which bone is held steady has.

    Muscle actions and opposing groups

    A muscle can only shorten and pull. It can’t push a bone back where it came from. So every movement that one muscle produces has to be reversed by a different muscle on the opposite side of the joint. That is why muscles are arranged in opposing groups.

    Four roles in every movement

    Agonist (prime mover): produces most of the movement.
    Antagonist: opposes the movement; it relaxes and lengthens, and it can brake the motion.
    Synergist: assists the agonist or cancels an unwanted extra motion.
    Fixator: holds the origin’s bone steady so the agonist pulls efficiently.

    Roles change with the movement. The triceps is the antagonist in a curl but the agonist in a push-up.

    Movement vocabulary refresher

    Flexion decreases the angle between bones; extension increases it. Abduction moves a limb away from the midline; adduction moves it toward the midline. At the ankle, dorsiflexion lifts the top of the foot toward the shin and plantar flexion points the toes down. Rotation turns a bone around its own long axis.

    Use position to predict action

    You don’t have to memorize every action. A muscle pulls its insertion toward its origin, so the movement happens toward the side of the joint the muscle crosses. Find the side, and the action follows.

    Watch the knee. The knee bends backward, so its rule is the reverse of the elbow’s. During development the lower limb rotates medially, which puts the knee’s extensors (quadriceps) in front and its flexors (hamstrings) behind. A muscle that crosses two joints, like rectus femoris, acts on both.
