Plate Tectonics Lab

Earth Science 101, Unit 4: Earth’s interior and plate tectonics

Earth’s rigid outer shell is broken into plates that move a few centimeters a year. Where they meet, they build mountains, open oceans, and trigger earthquakes and eruptions. Work through the six stations in order, or jump to the one your instructor assigns.

What you’ll be able to do
  • Distinguish Earth’s compositional layers (crust, mantle, core) from its mechanical layers (lithosphere, asthenosphere). Station 1
  • Relate divergent, convergent, and transform boundaries to the features they produce, and draw their cross-sections. Station 2
  • Explain how seafloor age, magnetic patterns, earthquake distributions, and GPS measurements show that plates move. Stations 2, 3, 5
  • Identify plate boundaries from maps of earthquakes, volcanoes, and seafloor features, and predict their geologic activity. Station 4
  • Calculate plate velocity from distance and elapsed time. Stations 3, 5
Instructor guide (50–75 minute lab)
  • Station 1 (10 min): Students drag the depth probe and record layer, temperature, and state at 50, 200, 3,000, and 6,000 km. Ask why the inner core is solid while the outer core is liquid.
  • Station 2 (15 min): For each boundary, students turn labels off, pause, and sketch the cross-section with motion arrows and earthquake depths, then turn labels on to self-check. Each boundary ends with a prediction question.
  • Station 3 (10 min): Build 6 million years of seafloor, then solve the mystery ridge (calculate spreading rate from a stripe boundary).
  • Station 4 (15 min, pairs): Identify the five hidden boundaries using map evidence only. Discuss which single data layer was most diagnostic.
  • Station 5 (10 min): Read velocity as the slope of GPS data, then complete three practice problems.
  • Station 6 (exit ticket): Ten-question auto-graded check with explanations, plus flip-card vocabulary.

Inside Earth: two ways to slice the planet

Geologists describe Earth’s layers by what they are made of (composition) and by how they respond to stress (mechanical behavior). Switch views, select a layer, and drag the depth probe toward the center.

The top 700 km is stretched so the thin crust, lithosphere, and asthenosphere are visible. Everything deeper is drawn to scale.

How do the two views line up? The lithosphere is the crust plus the rigid top of the mantle, so a plate is more than crust. The asthenosphere sits inside the upper mantle. A layer can be solid rock and still flow slowly, the way glacier ice does.

Seafloor evidence: magnetic stripes and crust age

As new crust cools at a ridge, magnetic minerals lock in the direction of Earth’s magnetic field. The field flips from time to time, so the seafloor records a striped history on both sides of the ridge. Press play to build 6 million years of seafloor.

What to notice

  • The stripe pattern on one side is a mirror image of the other.
  • Crust is youngest at the ridge and gets older in both directions.
  • A faster ridge makes wider stripes for the same span of time.

Evidence checklist
  • Are earthquakes only shallow, or do they get deeper in one direction?
  • Are there volcanoes? In a line parallel to a trench?
  • Is there a ridge, a trench, an offset, or a mountain belt?
  • Is the site in an ocean, at a coast, or inside a continent?

Plate speed: from measurements to velocity

Velocity equals distance divided by elapsed time. GPS stations on either side of a boundary measure plate motion directly, a few millimeters at a time. The slope of their data is the velocity.
