How do biologists know what they know?

Life is organized in layers, from atoms to the whole planet, and scientists study every layer with the same tool: careful, testable questions. This lesson gives you both.

By the end, you will be able to:

  • Describe the characteristics of life and how living systems are organized from cells to the biosphere.
  • Formulate a testable hypothesis, identify variables, and choose an appropriate control.
  • Draw a conclusion from a graph or data table, weighing replication, evidence, and uncertainty.

What counts as alive?

Biologists don’t define life with a single rule. Instead, living things share a set of characteristics, and something is considered alive when it shows all of them.

A fire grows, uses oxygen, and can spread, yet it isn’t alive. A dormant seed seems to do nothing for years, yet it is. The difference lies in the full list below: no single trait is enough, but together they mark the boundary between living and nonliving.

Levels of biological organization

Life is organized in a hierarchy. Each level is built from the level below it, and new abilities, called emergent properties, appear at each step up.

Emergent property: a property that a level has but its individual parts do not. A single heart muscle cell can contract, but only a whole heart can pump blood.

      What makes a hypothesis testable?

      A hypothesis is a proposed, testable explanation. It is not a guess about anything at all: it has to be something an experiment or observation could show to be wrong. A testable hypothesis names a factor you can change or measure and an outcome you can measure.

      Hypothesis vs. prediction. The hypothesis is the explanation (“Light powers photosynthesis, so more light produces more growth”). The prediction is the specific result you expect if the hypothesis is true (“If plants get 12 hours of light, then they will be taller after 21 days than plants that get 4 hours”).

      Designing an experiment

      A fair test changes one factor on purpose, measures one outcome, and keeps everything else the same.

      • Independent variable: the factor you deliberately change.
      • Dependent variable: the outcome you measure, which may depend on the independent variable.
      • Controlled variables: conditions you keep the same in every group so they can’t explain the results.
      • Control group: a group that does not receive the treatment, giving you a baseline for comparison.
      • Replication: repeating the treatment on many individuals or trials so one unusual result can’t mislead you.

        Evidence, data, and uncertainty

        Data never come out perfectly. Good scientists show how much their measurements vary and say how confident they are, using words like “support” rather than “prove.”

        When you read a graph, find the independent variable on the x‑axis and the dependent variable on the y‑axis. Then look for the overall pattern, the highest and lowest points, and the error bars. An error bar shows the range where the true average probably lies. When the error bars of two groups overlap a lot, the difference between them could easily be due to chance.
