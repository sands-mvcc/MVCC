Part 1

Why password security still matters

Stolen and guessed credentials remain one of the most common ways attackers get in. As a security professional you will audit password hashes, write and enforce password policy, investigate login attacks, and help users choose better. By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. Define core terms: entropy, keyspace, hash, salt, pepper, key stretching, and the major password attack types.
  2. Calculate keyspace, entropy, and estimated crack time for a password under different storage and attack conditions.
  3. Explain how systems store passwords and why unique salts and slow, memory-hard hashing defeat offline attacks.
  4. Demonstrate dictionary, rule-based, mask, and brute-force attacks in an authorized lab and interpret the results.
  5. Evaluate passwords and password policies against NIST SP 800-63B and recommend improvements, including MFA, password managers, and passkeys.
  6. Identify password spraying and credential stuffing from indicators and choose countermeasures.
  7. Apply legal and ethical boundaries to password auditing and the handling of cracked credentials.

  • Explain password risk to non-technical users and leaders

    Part 4

    Guessing odds: average case versus human nature

    Keyspace math gives an average. A brute-force attacker finds a truly random secret, on average, halfway through the keyspace. But people do not choose randomly, and attackers order their guesses to exploit that. Simulate attacks on 4-digit PINs and compare what happens with what the math predicts.

    Part 5

    Hashing, salting, and key stretching

    Systems should never store your password. They store a hash: a one-way fingerprint. At login, the system hashes what you type and compares fingerprints. These tools compute real SHA-256 hashes in your browser.

    Key stretching: make every guess expensive

    Fast hashes are great for files and terrible for passwords. Key stretching repeats the work thousands of times. You pay the cost once per login; the attacker pays it for every guess. Time it in your own browser.

    Simple iteration demonstrates the idea. Production systems use purpose-built functions: Argon2id (memory-hard, OWASP’s first choice), scrypt, bcrypt, or PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 at roughly 600,000 iterations. A pepper is a secret added to every password and stored apart from the database, such as in a hardware security module, so a stolen database alone is not enough to start cracking.

      Part 9

      What a strength meter cannot tell you

      Every number in this lab assumes the attacker has to guess. Many real attacks never guess at all. Open each item to see where strength estimates break down.

      Entropy formulas assume randomness

      L × log₂(N) is only true when every character is chosen at random. People pick words, names, years, and keyboard patterns, so real entropy is far lower. That is why the lab’s pattern-aware estimate is often millions of times smaller than the brute-force math. Estimators like zxcvbn model these patterns; the one in this lab is a simplified teaching version.

      Reuse and credential stuffing

      A perfect password reused on two sites is only as safe as the weaker site’s storage. When one site is breached, attackers replay the username and password pairs everywhere else. Uniqueness matters as much as strength, and password managers make uniqueness practical.

      Phishing, keyloggers, and MFA fatigue

      A phishing page or keylogger captures the password itself, so strength is irrelevant. Push-notification MFA can be defeated by bombarding users with prompts. Phishing-resistant methods such as FIDO2 security keys and passkeys bind the login to the real site and do not hand over a reusable secret.

      Passkeys and passwordless authentication

      A passkey replaces the password with a public-private key pair. The server stores only the public key, so a database breach yields nothing to crack, and the private key never leaves the user’s device. Expect to support both passwords and passkeys for years.

      Screening against breached passwords

      NIST SP 800-63B requires checking new passwords against a blocklist of commonly used, expected, or compromised values. Services such as Have I Been Pwned’s Pwned Passwords use k-anonymity: the system sends only the first five characters of the password’s SHA-1 hash and checks the returned list locally, so the password is never revealed. The lab’s blocklist has only a few hundred entries; real lists hold hundreds of millions.

      Speeds change, so estimates age

      The speeds in this lab are approximate single-GPU figures in the range of published hashcat benchmarks for a current high-end consumer card. Each hardware generation is faster, and attackers rent GPUs by the hour. Treat crack times as orders of magnitude, re-check benchmarks when you teach, and choose storage settings with headroom.

      Law, ethics, and handling cracked credentials
      • Crack only hashes you are explicitly authorized in writing to test. Unauthorized access can violate the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and state laws, even with good intentions.
      • Follow the rules of engagement: scope, dates, systems, and what to do with sensitive findings.
      • Never use recovered passwords to log in to anything outside scope, including users’ personal accounts.
      • Report results in aggregate where possible, store evidence encrypted, and destroy cracked credentials when the engagement ends.
      • Recommend fixes for the system, not punishment for users.

      Assessment ideas
      • Exit ticket: in two sentences without jargon, explain to a coworker why Summer2026! is weak and what to do instead.
      • Lab practical: in an isolated virtual lab, crack an instructor-provided hash file with hashcat or John the Ripper. Submit the percentage cracked by each attack type, the hash mode used, and three prioritized recommendations.
      • Policy memo: rewrite a provided legacy password policy (8 characters, complexity, 90-day rotation) to align with NIST SP 800-63B, with a one-paragraph rationale for leadership.
      • Rubric dimensions: calculation accuracy, correct attack and hash-mode selection, interpretation of results, standards alignment, plain-language clarity, ethical handling of credentials.
      Extending into a hands-on cracking lab
      • Use an isolated network or offline VMs and instructor-generated hashes only. Never use real user data.
      • Progress students from -a 0 (wordlist) to -r rules to -a 3 masks, mirroring Part 6.
      • Have students identify hash formats by structure, for example $2b$12$ for bcrypt, before choosing a mode.
      • Pair with a defensive task: enable a banned-password list and smart lockout, then review Windows Event ID 4625 and 4771 logs after a simulated spray.
      Accessibility and adaptation
      • Every result is labeled in text, not just color, and the page works fully with a keyboard.
      • Atkinson Hyperlegible and its mono variant clearly distinguish look-alike characters such as 0/O and 1/l/I, which matters when reading passwords and hashes.
      • Animations respect the reduced-motion setting. Long-running attacks can be stopped at any time.
      • For networking programs, extend with RADIUS and 802.1X credential handling; for programming students, have them implement Argon2id storage in their language of choice.
