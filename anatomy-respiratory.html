The nervous system and special senses

The brain and spinal cord, the nerves that connect them to the body, the cells that do the work, and two sense organs: the eye and the ear.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Distinguish central nervous system (CNS) structures from peripheral nervous system (PNS) structures.
  • Identify the major regions and lobes of the brain and state a main function of each.
  • Describe the cross-sectional anatomy of the spinal cord and how spinal nerves form.
  • Identify the parts of a neuron and match each type of neuroglia to its location and job.
  • Identify the structures of the eye and trace the path of light to the retina.
  • Identify the structures of the ear and trace sound vibrations to the cochlea.

The big division. The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and spinal cord, the body’s command center. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is everything else: cranial nerves, spinal nerves, their branches, ganglia, and sensory receptors. The PNS carries information into and out of the CNS.

Reading the diagrams

Brain diagrams use two standard views. A lateral view shows the left side of the brain, with the front (anterior) at the left of the picture. A midsagittal view shows the brain cut down the middle between the hemispheres, exposing deep structures like the thalamus and corpus callosum.

The eye and ear are shown in section, as if sliced through, so you can see the inner layers and chambers.

How the PNS is organized by function

The PNS has a sensory (afferent) division that carries signals to the CNS and a motor (efferent) division that carries commands away from it. The motor division splits again by what it controls.

  • Nervous system
    • CNSBrain and spinal cord; integration and command
    • PNSCranial and spinal nerves, ganglia
      • Sensory (afferent)From receptors to the CNS
      • Motor (efferent)From the CNS to effectors
        • SomaticVoluntary; skeletal muscle
        • AutonomicInvoluntary; cardiac and smooth muscle, glands
          • Sympathetic“Fight or flight”
          • Parasympathetic“Rest and digest”

Same tissue, different names. A bundle of axons is a nerve in the PNS but a tract in the CNS. A cluster of neuron cell bodies is a ganglion in the PNS but a nucleus in the CNS.

Gray matter, white matter, and the protective coverings

In the cerebrum, gray matter (neuron cell bodies) forms the thin outer cerebral cortex, and white matter (myelinated axons) lies beneath it. The spinal cord is the reverse: gray matter inside, white matter outside.

The brain and cord are wrapped in three meninges: the tough dura mater, the web-like arachnoid mater, and the delicate pia mater that hugs every gyrus. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), made mostly by the choroid plexuses in the ventricles, circulates in the subarachnoid space and cushions the CNS.

31 pairs of spinal nerves

Named for the region where they leave the vertebral column: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, and 1 coccygeal. Every spinal nerve is mixed, carrying both sensory and motor axons.

In adults the cord itself ends near the L1–L2 vertebrae. Below that, a bundle of nerve roots called the cauda equina (“horse’s tail”) continues down the canal.

12 pairs of cranial nerves

Cranial nerves leave the brain directly, mostly from the brainstem. They are part of the PNS. Some are purely sensory (olfactory, optic, vestibulocochlear), some mainly motor, and some mixed.

Neurons and glia

Nervous tissue has two kinds of cells. Neurons carry electrical signals. Neuroglia (glial cells) support, protect, and insulate them, and outnumber neurons in the CNS.

Six types of neuroglia

Four live in the CNS and two in the PNS. Watch for the pair that makes myelin: one in each division.

Classifying neurons by structure and function

By structure: Multipolar neurons have many dendrites and one axon; they are the most common type and include motor neurons and interneurons. Bipolar neurons have one dendrite and one axon; they are rare and found in the retina and olfactory epithelium. Unipolar (pseudounipolar) neurons have a single short process that splits in two; most sensory neurons are unipolar, with their cell bodies in the dorsal root ganglia.

By function: Sensory (afferent) neurons carry signals from receptors toward the CNS. Motor (efferent) neurons carry commands from the CNS to muscles and glands. Interneurons lie entirely within the CNS and connect the two; they are by far the most numerous.

The eye

The wall of the eyeball has three layers: an outer fibrous layer (sclera and cornea), a middle vascular layer (choroid, ciliary body, iris), and an inner neural layer (retina).

    Rods, cones, and focusing

    Rods (about 120 million per eye) work in dim light and give black-and-white, low-detail vision; they are concentrated around the edges of the retina. Cones (about 6 million) need brighter light and provide color and sharp detail; they are packed into the fovea centralis.

    Light must pass through the retina’s ganglion and bipolar cell layers before reaching the rods and cones at the back. The image that lands on the retina is upside down and reversed; the brain flips it.

    Accommodation: to focus on something close, the ciliary muscle contracts, the suspensory ligaments slacken, and the elastic lens rounds up and bends light more. For distant objects the ciliary muscle relaxes and the lens flattens.

    The ear

    The ear has three parts. The external ear collects sound, the air-filled middle ear amplifies it, and the fluid-filled internal ear turns it into nerve signals. The internal ear also senses balance.

      Balance: the other job of the internal ear

      The vestibule holds two sacs, the utricle and saccule, whose hair cells sense head position and straight-line acceleration (static equilibrium). The three semicircular canals sit at right angles to one another; fluid moving inside them signals rotation of the head (dynamic equilibrium). Both send signals through the vestibular branch of cranial nerve VIII.
