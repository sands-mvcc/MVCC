The respiratory system

Upper and lower airways, the lungs and their lobes, the pleura, the bronchial tree, and the alveoli where oxygen enters the blood.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Identify the structures of the upper and lower respiratory tract on a model or diagram.
  • Describe the lobes and fissures of each lung and the arrangement of the pleura.
  • Describe how the bronchial tree branches and how its walls change along the way.
  • Trace airflow in order from the nostrils to the alveoli.
  • Explain how the alveolar–capillary arrangement creates a surface for gas exchange.

Two ways to divide the system. By location: the upper respiratory tract is the nose, nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, and pharynx; the lower respiratory tract is the larynx, trachea, bronchi, and lungs. (Some clinical sources place the larynx in the upper tract; this lesson follows the anatomy-textbook convention.)

By function: the conducting zone (nose through terminal bronchioles) moves, cleans, warms, and humidifies air but exchanges no gas. The respiratory zone (respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, alveolar sacs, and alveoli) is where gas exchange happens.

Breathing versus respiration

Pulmonary ventilation (breathing) moves air in and out of the lungs. External respiration is gas exchange between alveolar air and the blood in the lungs. Internal respiration is exchange between the blood and the body’s tissues. This lesson focuses on the anatomy that makes the first two possible.

Lungs, lobes, and pleura

Each lung is a cone-shaped organ with an apex rising just above the clavicle, a concave base resting on the diaphragm, and a hilum on its medial surface where the bronchi, blood vessels, nerves, and lymphatics enter and leave.

The pleura

Each lung sits in its own sac of pleura, a double-layered serous membrane. Picture pushing your fist into a partly inflated balloon: the layer touching your fist is the visceral pleura (on the lung), the outer layer is the parietal pleura (lining the chest wall), and the thin space between is the pleural cavity. The bronchial tree

From the trachea to the alveoli, the airway branches about 23 times. As the tubes get smaller, cartilage disappears, smooth muscle takes over, and the lining thins until it is just one flat cell thick. Step down the tree and watch the wall change.

      Gas exchange at the alveolus

      Every alveolus is wrapped in a net of pulmonary capillaries. Where they touch, air and blood are separated only by the respiratory membrane: the alveolar wall, a shared basement membrane, and the capillary wall. Gases cross it by simple diffusion, each moving from higher to lower partial pressure.

      Not to scale. The membrane is drawn many times thicker than life so its layers are visible.

      Select a part above or on the diagram.

      Why this arrangement works so well

      Huge surface. About 300–500 million alveoli give a total surface of roughly 70 m², close to the floor area of a small apartment, all folded into your chest.

      Tiny distance. The respiratory membrane averages about 0.5 µm thick, less than one-tenth the width of a red blood cell.

      Steep gradients. Alveolar air (PO₂ about 104 mmHg) meets oxygen-poor blood (PO₂ about 40 mmHg), so O₂ moves into the blood. CO₂ moves the other way, from about 45 to 40 mmHg.

      Matched flow. Capillaries are so dense they form a nearly continuous sheet of blood, and each red cell lingers long enough (about 0.75 s at rest) for exchange to finish.

      Experiment: what limits diffusion?

      The rate of diffusion across the membrane rises with surface area and the pressure difference, and falls as the membrane gets thicker (a simplified Fick’s law). Move the sliders, or try each condition below. Try all five to complete this section.
