The cardiovascular system

Heart chambers and valves, the great vessels, arteries and veins, and the exact path blood takes through the heart, lungs, and body.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Identify the four chambers, four valves, and great vessels on a heart model, specimen, or diagram.
  • Explain what the AV and semilunar valves do during each phase of the heartbeat.
  • Trace blood in order from the venae cavae through the heart, lungs, and body, and back again.
  • Compare the pulmonary and systemic circuits.
  • Distinguish arteries, veins, and capillaries by wall structure and by direction of flow.

Read diagrams like an anatomist. Heart diagrams show the heart from the front (anterior view), as if the patient were facing you. The patient’s right side is on your left.

About the colors. By convention, blue marks oxygen-poor blood and red marks oxygen-rich blood. Real blood is always red: oxygen-poor blood is dark red, oxygen-rich blood is bright red.

Before you explore: where the heart sits and what it’s made of

The heart is about the size of a closed fist. It sits in the mediastinum, the central chest compartment between the lungs, resting on the diaphragm. About two-thirds of its mass lies left of the body’s midline. Its broad base faces up and back toward the right shoulder, and its pointed apex points down, forward, and toward the left hip.

The heart is wrapped in the pericardium, a double-walled sac. A thin film of serous fluid between its layers lets the heart beat with little friction.

The heart wall has three layers: the outer epicardium, the thick muscular myocardium that does the pumping, and the inner endocardium, which lines the chambers and covers the valves.

Valves and the heartbeat

Four one-way valves keep blood moving forward. They open and close passively, pushed by pressure differences between chambers.

Atrioventricular (AV) valves

Between each atrium and its ventricle. The tricuspid (three cusps) is on the right; the mitral or bicuspid (two cusps) is on the left. Chordae tendineae and papillary muscles keep their cusps from flipping back into the atria.

Semilunar valves

At the exit of each ventricle. The pulmonary valve guards the right ventricle’s exit to the pulmonary trunk; the aortic valve guards the left ventricle’s exit to the aorta. Each has three half-moon cusps and no chordae.

Memory aid: “Try before you buy.” Blood passes the tricuspid (right side) before the bicuspid (left side).

See the valves work

Switch between the two phases of the heartbeat, or play the heart sounds and watch which valves close on each one.

Heart sounds: what you hear with a stethoscope

S1 (“lub”) is the sound of the AV valves (tricuspid and mitral) closing as the ventricles begin to contract. It marks the start of ventricular systole.

S2 (“dub”) is the sound of the semilunar valves (pulmonary and aortic) closing as the ventricles relax. It marks the start of ventricular diastole.

A murmur is an extra whooshing sound, often from blood leaking backward through a valve that doesn’t close fully or squeezing through one that doesn’t open fully.

    Two circuits, one heart

    The heart is really two pumps side by side. The right side pumps to the lungs; the left side pumps to everything else. Both pumps beat at the same moment.

    Pulmonary circuitSystemic circuit
    PumpRight ventricleLeft ventricle
    Leaves the heart throughPulmonary trunk and arteriesAorta
    Exchange happens inLung capillaries around the alveoliCapillaries in every body tissue
    What changesBlood picks up O2 and unloads CO2Blood delivers O2 and nutrients, picks up CO2 and wastes

    Both ventricles eject about the same volume with each beat (roughly 70 mL at rest). The left ventricle’s thicker wall reflects the higher pressure it must generate, not a larger volume.

    Arteries, veins, and capillaries

    Vessels are named by the direction blood travels, not by how much oxygen it carries. Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood toward it.

    Blood leaving a ventricle always moves through vessels in this order:

    1. Arteries
    2. Arterioles
    3. Capillaries
    4. Venules
    5. Veins

    Major vessels to know

    Great vessels at the heart

    Superior and inferior venae cavae return oxygen-poor blood from the body to the right atrium. Pulmonary trunk leaves the right ventricle and divides into the right and left pulmonary arteries. The pulmonary veins (usually four) return oxygen-rich blood from the lungs to the left atrium. The aorta leaves the left ventricle.

    The aorta and its first branches

    The ascending aorta gives off the right and left coronary arteries, which feed the heart muscle. The aortic arch gives off three branches, from right to left: the brachiocephalic trunk (which splits into the right common carotid and right subclavian arteries), the left common carotid artery, and the left subclavian artery. The descending aorta continues as the thoracic aorta and, below the diaphragm, the abdominal aorta.

    Veins of the heart wall

    The heart’s own veins (cardiac veins) collect into the coronary sinus on the posterior surface, which empties into the right atrium.
