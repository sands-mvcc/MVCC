The big idea: moving heat, not making it

A residential air-source heat pump uses electricity to move heat between the home and the outdoor air. In winter it absorbs heat outdoors and releases it indoors. In summer it absorbs heat indoors and releases it outdoors.

The system in this module is a ducted split system: an outdoor unit, an indoor air handler, and two refrigerant lines connecting them — a larger vapor line and a smaller liquid line.

Even cold outdoor air holds heat. Refrigerant boiling in the outdoor coil is colder than the air around it, so heat flows into the refrigerant. The compressor then raises its pressure and temperature high enough to release that heat indoors.

Tracing the circuit
The compressor always takes in low-pressure vapor and discharges high-pressure vapor. The reversing valve changes which coil receives that discharge.

That one fact is the easiest way to trace the circuit in either mode. Start at the compressor discharge and follow the hot gas:

  1. Which coil does the reversing valve send discharge vapor to? That coil is the condenser — it releases heat.
  2. The condensed liquid flows through the liquid line to a metering device, which drops its pressure and temperature.
  3. The other coil is the evaporator — the refrigerant boils there and absorbs heat.
  4. The vapor returns through the reversing valve to compressor suction.

Metering arrangements vary by model. Many split heat pumps use a metering device at each coil, with a check or bypass feature so refrigerant passes freely in the direction where that device isn’t metering. The simulator shows that arrangement.

Gauge safety on heat pumps. In heating, the large vapor line between the units carries hot, high-pressure discharge vapor — not suction. Know the mode before you connect gauges or touch lines.
Heating

Hot refrigerant vapor flows from the compressor through the reversing valve to the indoor coil, where it releases heat to the supply air and condenses. The liquid passes through the metering device, dropping in pressure and temperature. It then enters the outdoor coil, where it absorbs heat from outdoor air and evaporates before returning to the compressor.

Heat-pump capacity falls as outdoor temperature drops, because there’s less heat available in the outdoor air and the compressor has to work across a larger pressure difference. Watch the supply-air temperature fall as you lower the outdoor temperature slider.

Cooling

The reversing valve sends hot discharge vapor to the outdoor coil, which releases heat outdoors. After metering, the refrigerant enters the indoor coil, where it absorbs heat from return air.

Moisture in the return air condenses on the cold indoor coil, drips into the condensate pan, and drains away. A blocked drain can back water up into the pan; many installations include a float switch that shuts the system off before the pan overflows.

Defrost

In heating mode the outdoor coil runs below freezing much of the winter, so frost builds up on it. The controls briefly switch the refrigerant circuit to cooling operation so hot refrigerant warms the outdoor coil and melts the frost. The system then returns to heating.

On many designs the outdoor fan stops during defrost so the heat stays in the coil, and backup heat comes on to temper the supply air. Even so, briefly cooler supply air can occur during defrost. Ice that persists and blocks airflow calls for service.

With electric heat strips, extended backup operation generally uses more electricity than heat-pump operation. A customer who sees backup heat running often in mild weather may have a heat pump that isn’t delivering full capacity.

Reading the system

A technician reads the system through a handful of numbers. The simulator shows all of them.

ReadingWhat it tells you
Suction pressure and its saturation temperatureThe boiling temperature in the evaporator. Low airflow or a low charge pulls it down.
Discharge pressure and its saturation temperatureThe condensing temperature. Poor airflow across the condenser (dirty coil, failed fan) drives it up.
SuperheatHow far the suction vapor is above its boiling temperature. High superheat often points to an underfed evaporator, such as a low charge.
SubcoolingHow far the liquid is below its condensing temperature. Low subcooling often accompanies a low charge.
Temperature split (return minus supply, or rise in heating)Whether the system is moving heat in the expected direction and roughly the expected amount.
About the simulator’s numbers. Readings are training approximations built on an R-410A pressure–temperature relationship. Newer equipment may use A2L refrigerants such as R-454B or R-32, with different pressures and handling requirements. On real equipment, evaluate charge only with the manufacturer’s procedure and charts.
Required maintenance
Who and whenWhat to check
Homeowner, routinelyCheck the return-air filter and clean or replace it according to the equipment and filter instructions. Keep supply registers and return grilles clear. Remove leaves, vegetation, and loose snow around the outdoor unit, maintaining the manufacturer’s specified clearances.
Homeowner, during seasonal operationWatch for water around the indoor air handler in cooling season and persistent ice on the outdoor coil in heating season. Note unusual noise, weak airflow, or frequent backup heat so you can describe the symptom during service.
HVAC technician, typically annually or per the manufacturerInspect coils, fans, blower, filter, drain pan and line, electrical connections, and controls. Verify airflow, check for signs of refrigerant leakage, and evaluate charge using the manufacturer’s procedure when indicated. Check reversing-valve operation, defrost controls, and backup heat if installed.

Filter intervals depend on the equipment and household conditions. The U.S. Department of Energy’s Building Science Education resource gives about 90 days as a starting point for many ducted systems and directs owners to follow the manufacturer’s instructions.

Electrical testing and sealed refrigerant circuit service belong to a qualified technician. In the U.S., servicing that could release refrigerant requires EPA Section 608 certification.

The operating check

  1. Start the system from the thermostat.
  2. Let it stabilize.
  3. Verify airflow and the expected direction of the return-to-supply temperature change.
  4. Check condensate removal during cooling.
  5. Verify the other mode, defrost, and backup heat as applicable, using the manufacturer’s test procedures and outdoor-temperature limits.
  6. Record the operating conditions and readings so the next visit has a baseline.
