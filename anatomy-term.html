    Part 1

    Anatomical terminology and body organization

    Before you can describe a structure, you need a fixed reference pose, a set of directions, and a way to name cuts through the body.

    1.1

    Anatomical position and body regions

    The reference pose behind every anatomical description

    Anatomists describe every structure as if the body were in anatomical position: standing upright and facing forward, feet flat and slightly apart, arms at the sides with the palms facing forward and thumbs pointing away from the body. A patient lying on a table or curled on their side is still described as if they were in this pose, so descriptions never change when the body moves.

    Right and left always mean the subject’s right and left. When you face someone in anatomical position, their right side is on your left. Regional names come from Latin roots you’ll meet again and again: brachium (arm), carpus (wrist), femur (thigh), crus (leg).

    1.2

    Directional terms

    Paired words that describe where one structure is relative to another

    Directional terms come in opposing pairs, and they are always relative. “The nose is superior to the mouth” is meaningful; “the nose is superior” is not. Each sentence you write should name two structures and one direction.

    In humans, anterior and ventral mean the same thing (the belly side), as do posterior and dorsal. Proximal and distal are reserved for limbs and branching structures: they measure distance from the point of attachment, not from the head.

    1.3

    Planes and sections

    Naming the cuts you’ll see in models, dissections, CT and MRI

    A plane is an imaginary flat surface passing through the body; a section is the cut surface you see along that plane. Medical images are sections, so naming the plane is the first step in reading any scan or sectioned model.

    Three planes sit at right angles to each other. The sagittal plane separates right from left; the frontal (coronal) plane separates anterior from posterior; the transverse (horizontal) plane separates superior from inferior. Any cut at another angle is oblique.

    1.4

    Body cavities and abdominal regions

    Where the organs live, and how clinicians map the abdomen

    The dorsal body cavity protects the nervous system: the cranial cavity holds the brain and the vertebral canal holds the spinal cord. The larger ventral body cavity holds the internal organs (viscera). The dome-shaped diaphragm divides it into the thoracic cavity above and the abdominopelvic cavity below.

    Inside the ventral cavity, organs are wrapped in thin, double-layered serous membranes. The parietal layer lines the cavity wall and the visceral layer covers the organ: the pleura around the lungs, the pericardium around the heart, and the peritoneum in the abdomen.

    1.5

    Levels of structural organization

    From atoms to a whole person

    The body is built in levels, and each level is made of units from the level below. Atoms form molecules; molecules form cells, the smallest units of life; similar cells and the material around them form tissues; two or more tissue types form an organ; organs that work together form an organ system; and all systems together form the organism. Part 2 of this lesson lives at the cell and tissue levels.

    Part 2

    Cells and histology

    Histology is the study of tissues. With a few rules about shape, layering and matrix, you can identify most tissues from a single microscope field.

    2.1

    Cell specialization

    Structure predicts function

    Your body contains roughly 200 kinds of cells, and nearly all of them carry the same DNA. They differ because each cell type switches on a different set of genes, producing a shape and internal machinery matched to one job. This is the most useful habit in histology: when you see an unusual shape, ask what job it serves.

    2.2

    The four tissue types

    Every organ is built from epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous tissue

    A tissue is a group of similar cells, plus the material between them, working together. Most slides you’ll study are stained with H&E, two dyes that color different chemistry:

    Hematoxylin stains nuclei blue-purple Eosin stains cytoplasm and protein fibers pink Unstained areas are empty space, fat or mucus

    2.4

    Tissue identification challenge

    Name the tissue, then justify it with visible evidence

    A correct name is only half an identification. In lab and on exams, you’ll be asked which features you can see that support your call. Use this decision path on every unknown slide:

    1. Is there a free surface with cells packed edge to edge? It’s epithelium. Count the layers, then check the shape of the surface cells.
    2. Are cells scattered in lots of extracellular matrix? It’s connective tissue. Read the matrix: loose fibers, packed fibers, glassy gel, hard rings or fluid.
    3. Are the cells long fibers? It’s muscle. Check for striations, nucleus position and branching.
    4. Large cell bodies with long processes among many small nuclei? It’s nervous tissue.
