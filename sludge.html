How solids treatment works

The liquid side produces two sludges: primary sludge from the primary clarifiers and waste activated sludge from the secondary process. Solids handling reduces their volume, stabilizes them, recovers energy, and produces a biosolids product that can be hauled away.

Thickening

Sludges are mostly water, so the first goal is removing water cheaply. Primary sludge settles well and is thickened in a gravity thickener, where a slow picket-fence rake helps it compact from about 3% to 5–6% solids. WAS is light and fluffy, so it is conditioned with polymer and drained on a gravity belt thickener from under 1% to about 5%.

Solids capture (%) = solids in thickened sludge ÷ solids fed × 100

Thicker feed means less volume in the digester and a longer residence time. Over-thick feed can overload digester biology; thin feed shortens residence time.

Anaerobic digestion

In a sealed, heated, mixed tank, bacteria break down volatile solids in three steps: hydrolysis, acidogenesis (producing volatile acids) and methanogenesis (converting acids to methane and CO₂). Acid formers are fast and tough. Methane formers are slow and sensitive to temperature changes, low pH and toxics.

VA/ALK ratio = volatile acids ÷ alkalinity. Below about 0.1–0.2 is healthy; above about 0.3–0.4 means trouble.
HRT (days) = digester volume ÷ daily feed volume. Without recycle, SRT equals HRT.

When feed outpaces the methane formers, acids accumulate, alkalinity is consumed, pH falls, and the digester goes sour. Recovery takes days to weeks, so operators watch VA/ALK and gas quality as early warnings. Holding temperature steady (within about 1 °C per day) matters as much as the setpoint.

Biogas handling and safety

Digester gas is roughly 60–65% methane. It is scrubbed of hydrogen sulfide, stored in a membrane holder, and used to heat the digester through a boiler and to run a combined heat and power (CHP) engine. Excess gas goes to a waste-gas flare. If storage still rises, a pressure relief valve vents raw gas, which is a safety hazard and a greenhouse gas release.

Digester buildings are hazardous locations (NFPA 820). Combustible gas detectors read in percent of the lower explosive limit (LEL). Ventilation starts on a low alarm. At a higher level, an independent safety instrumented system closes the gas isolation valve and shuts the boiler down. Because the SIS has its own sensors, logic solver and valves, a compromised PLC or HMI cannot stop it from acting.

Dewatering and biosolids

Digested sludge is conditioned with polymer and spun in a decanter centrifuge. The bowl throws solids to the wall; a scroll turning slightly slower than the bowl (the differential speed) conveys them out as cake at roughly 20–30% solids. Clear centrate returns to the plant. Every percentage point of cake dryness reduces the tonnage trucked away.

In the United States, 40 CFR Part 503 defines Class B biosolids. One recognized route is anaerobic digestion with a mean cell residence time of at least 15 days at 35–55 °C, with at least 38% volatile solids reduction for vector attraction reduction. Verify the exact requirements with your state program.

Guided exercises
  1. At 360×, let the plant run three days. Identify where solids leave the system, using the Solids balance tab.
  2. Raise the gravity thickener pump to 28 gpm. Explain what happens to the digester HRT and VS reduction over the next week.
  3. Cut GBT polymer to 3 lb/DT. Where do the lost solids go, and what does that do to the liquid side?
  4. Start a grease slug. Watch VA/ALK. Use sodium bicarbonate to keep pH up while the digester works through it.
  5. Disable the CHP and lower the flare setpoint. Compare energy recovery and gas wasted.
  6. Lower the centrifuge differential to 3 rpm. Find the best trade-off between cake dryness and torque.
  7. Inject the gas leak fault and follow the SIS response. Why does heating stop, and what must happen before reset?
  8. Launch the remote session attack and run for a week. How long until Class B is lost? Which defense would have caught it?
