Part 1

Why genetics belongs in your clinical toolkit

Genetic information now shows up in newborn screening results, prenatal visits, blood bank work, family histories, and medication choices. You will not be the genetic counselor, but you will often be the first person a patient asks. By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  1. Define core terms: gene, allele, genotype, phenotype, homozygous, heterozygous, dominant, recessive, and carrier.
  2. Construct monohybrid, dihybrid, X-linked, and codominant Punnett squares from parent genotypes.
  3. Calculate offspring probabilities and express them as fractions, percentages, and ratios.
  4. Distinguish autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant, X-linked recessive, codominant, and semidominant inheritance using real conditions.
  5. Explain recurrence risk in plain language, including that the risk applies independently to every pregnancy.
  6. Recognize what a Punnett square cannot predict and when to refer a patient to a genetics professional.

Knowledge, skills, and abilities for health care entry

KKnowledge

  • Mendel’s laws of segregation and independent assortment
  • Chromosomes, genes, alleles, and autosomes versus sex chromosomes
  • Major inheritance patterns and how each looks in a family
  • Common single-gene conditions: cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, Huntington disease, hemophilia A, familial hypercholesterolemia
  • ABO blood group inheritance for lab and transfusion work
  • Purpose of newborn, carrier, and prenatal screening
  • Protections under HIPAA and the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA)

SSkills

  • Build a Punnett square accurately from stated genotypes
  • Convert results among fractions, ratios, and percentages
  • Collect a three-generation family history and read a basic pedigree
  • Translate risk into plain language and confirm understanding with teach-back
  • Document genetic information accurately in the health record
  • Spot red flags that call for referral

AAbilities

  • Reason about probability and uncertainty without overstating certainty
  • Communicate sensitive results with empathy and cultural humility
  • Stay nondirective: support the patient’s own decisions
  • Protect confidentiality of family genetic information
  • Know the limits of your scope of practice
  • Connect genetic findings to the care plan and patient education

    Part 7

    What a Punnett square cannot tell you

    The square assumes one gene, two alleles per person, and predictable dominance. Real patients are messier. Open each item to see where the model breaks down.

    Reduced penetrance and variable expressivity

    Some people who inherit a disease-causing variant never show symptoms (reduced penetrance), and people with the same variant can have mild or severe disease (variable expressivity). BRCA1 variants raise cancer risk substantially but do not guarantee cancer. The square predicts genotype, not destiny.

    New (de novo) variants

    A child can have a dominant condition even when neither parent does, because the variant arose new in an egg or sperm. Achondroplasia is the classic example: most cases occur in children of average-stature parents. A square built from the parents’ genotypes would predict 0%.

    Polygenic and multifactorial traits

    Height, type 2 diabetes, most heart disease, and many birth defects result from many genes plus environment. These cannot be modeled with a single square. Family history still matters, but risk is estimated from population data rather than Mendelian ratios.

    Linked genes and mitochondrial inheritance

    Genes close together on a chromosome tend to be inherited together, so the 9:3:3:1 dihybrid ratio does not hold. Mitochondrial DNA passes from the mother to all of her children, a pattern no Punnett square shows.

    Communicating risk responsibly
    • Give risk two ways: “1 in 4, which is 25%.” Add the reverse: “3 in 4 chance the child will not have it.”
    • Stress that chance resets with each pregnancy.
    • Stay nondirective. Offer information and support; do not tell families what to decide.
    • Use teach-back: “To make sure I explained it clearly, how would you describe the risk to your partner?”
    Ethics, privacy, and referral
    • Genetic results are protected health information under HIPAA. A result can reveal information about relatives who did not consent to testing.
    • GINA (2008) restricts the use of genetic information by health insurers and employers, but does not cover life, disability, or long-term care insurance.
    • Predictive testing of minors for adult-onset conditions such as Huntington disease is generally discouraged by professional guidelines.
    • Refer to a genetic counselor when there is a known familial variant, multiple affected relatives, early onset of common disease, consanguinity, pregnancy planning concerns, or an abnormal screening result.

    • Exit ticket: write a two-sentence explanation of a 1 in 4 risk for a family whose first child has cystic fibrosis.
