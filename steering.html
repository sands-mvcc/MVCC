    Steering systems and their parts

    Every steering system turns the rotation of the steering wheel into side-to-side movement of the steering knuckles. Power assist only reduces the effort the driver needs; the mechanical linkage is always there as a backup.

    Steering gears

    Rack and pinion (nearly all cars and crossovers): a pinion gear on the end of the steering shaft meshes with teeth on a rack bar. Turning the pinion slides the rack left or right, and the tie rods push and pull the steering arms. It is light, direct, and has few joints to wear.

    Recirculating ball (many heavy trucks): a worm shaft drives a ball nut riding on steel balls; the nut turns a sector shaft and pitman arm, which moves a parallel linkage (center link, idler arm, tie rods). It handles heavy loads and has two adjustments: worm bearing preload and sector (over-center) lash.

    Linkage wear points: inner tie rods (ball sockets inside the rack boots), outer tie rod ends, the intermediate shaft U-joints, and the rack mounting bushings.

    Three ways to make assist

    Hydraulic power steering (HPS): an engine-driven pump supplies fluid flow; a rotary control valve in the pinion housing directs pressure to one side of a power piston on the rack.

    Electro-hydraulic (EHPS): the same hydraulic gear, but an electric motor drives the pump only when assist is needed, saving fuel.

    Electric power steering (EPS): no fluid at all. A torque sensor measures the driver’s effort and a module commands an electric motor on the column, pinion, or rack. EPS is now the most common design because it saves fuel and makes lane keeping, park assist, and automated steering possible.

    Whatever the design, a power steering failure should leave the driver with heavy but working manual steering. A complete loss of steering control always points to the mechanical linkage, not the assist system.

    Hydraulic power steering

    A hydraulic system is a loop: the pump pushes fluid to the control valve, the valve sends it to one side of the power piston, and the fluid returns through a cooler to the reservoir. Every hydraulic complaint is a problem with flow, pressure, or the valve that directs them.

    The pump, flow control, and relief valve

    Most systems use a belt-driven vane pump: a slotted rotor turns inside an oval cam ring, and sliding vanes trap fluid and carry it from the inlet to the outlet. Because pump output rises with engine speed, a flow control valve bypasses excess fluid back to the inlet so the gear receives roughly constant flow (about 2–3 gpm on many cars). A pressure relief valve, usually built into the flow control valve, limits maximum pressure (commonly about 1,000–1,500 psi; check the specification) when the wheels are held against the steering stops.

    The rotary control valve and torsion bar

    The steering shaft is connected to the pinion through a slim torsion bar. When the driver turns the wheel against tire resistance, the bar twists a few degrees. That twist rotates an inner spool relative to an outer sleeve, opening ports that send pressure to one side of the power piston and open the other side to return. Release the wheel and the bar untwists, centering the valve so pressure is equal on both sides. More twist means more pressure, so assist automatically matches effort.

    Hoses, cooler, and fluid

    The high-pressure hose (pump to gear) uses crimped fittings and must withstand relief pressure. The return hose is low pressure and often runs through a cooler loop in front of the radiator. Fluid type matters: systems may require ATF, a dedicated power steering fluid, or a synthetic such as CHF 11S. Mixing fluids can swell seals and cause foaming. Always use what the reservoir cap or service information specifies.

    Electric power steering

    EPS replaces the pump and fluid with a sensor, a computer, and a motor. That makes it efficient and smart, and it changes how you diagnose it: you test voltage, data, and calibrations instead of pressure and flow.

    How EPS decides how much to help

    A torque sensor on a torsion bar in the column or pinion measures how hard the driver is turning (non-contact magnetic or Hall-effect sensors are common). The EPS module reads that torque plus vehicle speed and engine or ready status over the network, looks up an assist map, and commands a brushless motor through a worm gear (column and pinion types) or a belt and ball nut (rack types).

    Assist is high at parking speeds and drops as speed rises for a stable highway feel. The module also adds active return to center, damping, and pull-drift compensation, and accepts steering requests from lane keeping and park assist.

    What EPS needs to work

    Power and ground: the motor can draw 60–100 A or more while parking, so a corroded ground or loose battery terminal causes low assist and voltage DTCs. Many systems reduce assist below about 10 V.

    Calibrations: after an alignment, column or rack replacement, or module replacement, the steering angle sensor and often the torque sensor zero point must be calibrated with a scan tool. A wrong zero point makes the vehicle pull with hands off.

    Thermal protection: repeated full-lock parking maneuvers heat the motor; the module temporarily reduces assist with no fault. That is normal.

    Before removing a steering wheel, column, or rack, center and lock the wheel and follow the SRS (airbag) disabling procedure and wait time. Turning a disconnected column can tear the clock spring.

    Dry-park check and free play lab

    With the vehicle on the ground (or the wheels on turntables), a helper rocks the steering wheel side to side about an inch while you watch each joint. A good joint moves with its neighbor. A worn joint shows one part moving before the other. Then measure free play: turn the wheel slowly until the tire just starts to move.

      Troubleshooting steering complaints

      Good diagnosticians test before they replace. A $400 steering rack does not fix a $20 belt, and a new pump will not fix a corroded ground on an EPS car.

      The diagnostic strategy

      1. Verify the concern. Road test and park-lot test: when is it hard, noisy, or loose? Cold or hot? One direction or both?
      2. Inspect. Tire pressures and sizes, fluid level and condition, belt, hoses, boots, and obvious linkage damage.
      3. Research. Service information, bulletins, DTCs, and data.
      4. Test and isolate. Effort test, analyzer test, dry-park check, voltage drop, or scan data. Pick the test that splits the possibilities.
      5. Find the root cause. A failed pump may have been starved by a leak. A failed EPS module may have been fed low voltage.
      6. Repair. Correct fluid, correct torques, correct bleed or calibration procedure.
      7. Verify. Road test, recheck level and leaks, check the steering wheel is centered, clear DTCs, and calibrate as required.

      Symptom chart

      Hard steering in both directions

      Low fluid, slipping belt, worn pump, stuck flow control or relief valve, restricted hose, low tire pressure, binding linkage or ball joints. EPS: low voltage, thermal protection, torque sensor or motor fault.

      Hard steering in one direction

      Internal leak in the rack (piston or valve seals) on that side, damaged rack, binding joint. Confirm with analyzer lock pressures.

      Hard when cold, normal when warm

      “Morning sickness”: worn rack housing bores where the valve seals ride. The leak shrinks as parts expand when warm. Usually requires a rack.

      Whine or groan

      Aerated fluid from low level or an air leak on the inlet side, wrong fluid, worn pump, restricted return. A brief groan at full lock is relief valve noise; do not hold at lock.

      Squeal when turning

      Glazed or worn belt, weak tensioner, misaligned pulley, or a seized pump loading the belt.

      Loose steering, wander, or clunk

      Worn inner or outer tie rods, rack bushings, intermediate shaft joints, excessive gear lash (recirculating ball), worn suspension. Use the dry-park check.

      Pull or drift

      Tires and alignment first. Then brake drag, a sticking rotary valve (HPS), or an uncalibrated torque sensor zero point (EPS).

      EPS warning lamp

      Read DTCs. Check battery state of charge, main power and ground voltage drop under load, network messages (vehicle speed), torque sensor and motor circuits, and required calibrations.
