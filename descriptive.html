Foundation

Why vary “said”? And when not to

“Said” is nearly invisible; readers glide past it. That neutrality is useful, but it gives up a chance to show emotion, urgency, and character in a single word.

A precise reporting verb does two jobs at once. It tags who is speaking and reveals how they speak, so you rarely need an adverb. “She whispered” beats “she said quietly.” Varied verbs also keep a long exchange from falling into a monotonous “he said, she said” rhythm.

The other half of the rule

Overdoing it is a recognized fault (editors call showy tags “said-bookisms”). If every line is hissed, growled, or ejaculated, the verbs upstage the dialogue. Use a descriptive verb when the manner of speaking matters; let “said” carry the routine lines. And never pair a verb with an adverb that repeats it: “whispered quietly” and “shouted loudly” say the same thing twice.

Why Hamlet is the perfect testing ground

A play has almost no reporting verbs. The script gives a speaker’s name and the words; the actor decides whether a line is pleaded or sneered. When you narrate a scene or write about it in an essay, you take the actor’s place. “Hamlet sneers” and “Hamlet laments” are two different arguments about the same line, and you need evidence for either one.

One convention to remember: in literary analysis, write in the present tense, because the text is always happening (“Claudius announces his marriage”). In a narrative retelling, use the past tense (“Claudius announced his marriage”).
