1

Map the communication process

Goal: identify the elements of the transactional model of communication in a real speaking situation.

Key idea. Communication isn’t a one-way broadcast. Speaker and audience send and receive at the same time, inside a context, while noise competes for attention. Good speakers manage every element, not just the words.
Maya, a nursing student, explains the steps of proper hand hygiene to classmates during a Tuesday evening class. She talks them through it, shows a slide, and demonstrates at the sink. A leaf blower roars outside the window, and one classmate keeps worrying about next period’s exam. Classmates nod, and one asks, “How long should we scrub?”

2

Check in on speaking nerves

Goal: recognize your own sources of communication apprehension and choose strategies that fit them.

Key idea. Most people feel some anxiety before speaking in public, including experienced professionals. Nerves are energy you can channel. This is a quick reflection, not a test or a diagnosis, and only you see your answers.
3

Sort the general purpose

Goal: distinguish speeches to inform, to persuade, and for special occasions.

Key idea. Every speech starts with a general purpose. Informative speeches increase understanding. Persuasive speeches change beliefs, attitudes, or actions. Special-occasion speeches mark a moment: a toast, a tribute, an award. Your purpose shapes everything that follows.

Put the speech in order

Goal: arrange the parts of an introduction, body, and conclusion into a logical structure.

Key idea. Audiences can’t reread. A clear structure (tell them what you’ll say, say it, tell them what you said) acts as a map. The introduction grabs attention and previews; the body develops main points linked by transitions; the conclusion summarizes and closes with impact.

    Experiment with delivery

    Goal: see how vocal emphasis changes meaning, and choose the right method of delivery.

    Key idea. How you say it matters as much as what you say. Vocal variety (rate, pitch, volume, emphasis, pauses) and nonverbal delivery (eye contact, gestures, posture) tell the audience what to pay attention to.

    Build and rehearse your speech of introduction

    Goal: apply purpose, structure, and delivery to draft and time a 2–3 minute speech.

    Key idea. Extemporaneous speaking means you prepare thoroughly and speak from brief notes rather than a script. Draft your outline in phrases, not paragraphs. Your work saves in this browser as you type.

    Rehearse with time signals

    Speech classes often use colored time cards. Green means you’ve reached the minimum, yellow means 30 seconds remain, red means time’s up. Have a partner tap the filler-word buttons while you practice.
