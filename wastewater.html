How the treatment train works

Pretreatment (screens and grit removal) and solids processing are outside this simulator. Flow arrives screened and de-gritted; sludge leaves to solids handling.

Primary treatment: gravity settling

Primary clarifiers slow the flow so heavy organic solids settle and grease floats. A rotating scraper pushes settled sludge to a center hopper, where the primary sludge pump draws it off on a timer. A well-run primary removes about 50–70% of suspended solids and 25–40% of BOD, which cuts the oxygen demand on the aeration basins.

Surface overflow rate (gpd/ft²) = flow (gpd) ÷ clarifier surface area (ft²)

Typical design is about 800–1,200 gpd/ft² at average flow. As SOR rises, particles have less time to settle and removal drops. If the sludge blanket is allowed to rise, solids carry over the weir and the sludge turns septic.

Secondary treatment: activated sludge

In the aeration basin, a suspension of bacteria (mixed liquor) consumes dissolved organics and, given enough oxygen and sludge age, converts ammonia to nitrate (nitrification). Blowers push air through fine-bubble diffusers. A PID loop trims blower output to hold dissolved oxygen near 2 mg/L: enough for the bacteria, without wasting energy. Aeration is often more than half of a plant’s electricity bill.

The secondary clarifier settles the biomass. Return activated sludge (RAS) pumps it back to the basin to keep the population up; waste activated sludge (WAS) removes the growth, which sets the sludge age.

SRT (days) = solids in system ÷ solids wasted per day
F/M = BOD load (lb/d) ÷ MLVSS under aeration (lb)
Solids loading (lb/d/ft²) = (Q + RAS) × MLSS × 8.34 ÷ clarifier area

Nitrifiers grow slowly, and slower still when cold, so winter operation usually needs a longer SRT. Filamentous bulking (high SVI) makes sludge settle poorly and can wash the blanket over the weirs.

Tertiary treatment: polishing and disinfection

Alum is dosed in proportion to flow to precipitate phosphorus, which helps prevent algae blooms downstream. Media filters catch the remaining floc and fine solids. As solids accumulate, headloss across the media rises until the PLC runs a backwash sequence: drain, air scour, backwash, then filter-to-waste rinse.

Sodium hypochlorite disinfects in a baffled contact tank. The baffles prevent short-circuiting so every parcel of water gets enough contact time. A residual analyzer feeds a PID loop that trims the flow-paced dose. Sodium bisulfite then removes the chlorine before discharge, because even 0.02 mg/L of residual chlorine is toxic to fish.

CT = chlorine residual (mg/L) × contact time (min). Higher CT means a higher log kill.

Unlike a discrete manufacturing line, a treatment plant is continuous. It responds over minutes to days, and its consequences reach the environment and public health. Chemical feed setpoints are high-value targets.

Guided exercises
  1. At 20×, watch one full diurnal cycle. When does the lag blower start, and why?
  2. Raise average flow to 6 MGD. What happens to SOR and primary removal? Which downstream stage feels it first?
  3. Drop the temperature to 10 °C and wait. When ammonia climbs, fix it with the WAS pump alone. Explain why it worked.
  4. On the DO faceplate, set Kp to 120 and Ti to 1 min. Describe the response on the Aeration trend, then retune it.
  5. Start a storm. Track the secondary clarifier blanket and E. coli. What could an operator change before the storm arrives?
  6. With every defense off, launch the chlorine setpoint attack. Which permit parameter fails, and how would the fish fare? Restore, enable one defense, and repeat.
  7. Launch the remote-access hijack with the firewall on. Why does the firewall not stop it?
  8. Complete three mystery fault challenges.
